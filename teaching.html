How I approach the classroom

Pedagogical
philosophy

Learning begins when students can see both the value of an idea and their place in the conversation around it.

I have spent much of my career alongside students who are negotiating more than a syllabus. Some are finding their way into higher education for the first time; others are balancing study with work and family. They arrive with knowledge shaped by those lives. I want the classroom to take that knowledge seriously, then invite students to test it against concepts and evidence that may stretch what they thought they knew.

Good teaching, for me, gives intellectual challenge a human shape. A difficult reading becomes more approachable when we pause over the question it is trying to answer. In a commerce classroom, a familiar business example can open a discussion about who benefits from a decision and whose experience the standard account leaves out. From there, students can return to the theory with sharper questions of their own. Dialogue matters because it allows us to hear an idea being formed, challenged and sometimes changed.

My research on decolonising curriculum practices deepens this commitment. It asks me to notice which forms of knowledge receive authority in our teaching and which are treated as illustrations at the margins. I want students to encounter African intellectual traditions as resources for serious analysis. This calls for careful choices about readings, examples and the questions we ask in class. It also requires humility from the educator: the curriculum should remain open to scrutiny, including my own assumptions about what counts as knowledge.

Students need a clear route through that work. I try to make expectations visible, break complex tasks into manageable stages and use feedback as a conversation about improvement. When a student struggles with an argument, the next useful question may be how the claim connects to its evidence. When a draft falls short, specific guidance can help the student return to it with greater confidence. Academic standards matter deeply to me. So does the patient work of helping people reach them, and the possibility that they may leave with a stronger sense of what their own thinking can do.
