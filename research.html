Understanding the conditions that shape curriculum, and the possibilities for changing them.

Doctoral inquiry

Research focus

Curriculum change is lived in classrooms, but its possibilities are shaped well beyond them.

My doctoral research asks how curriculum practices within South Africa’s private higher education institutions reproduce or resist coloniality. I am interested in the decisions that determine which knowledge is taught, whose intellectual traditions are granted authority and how lecturers work within the conditions their institutions create.

The study brings Critical Realism into conversation with decolonial scholarship. This allows me to look beneath visible curriculum choices and ask what gives them their shape: governance arrangements, ideas about educational value and the room educators have to act. A qualitative inquiry across three private institutions draws on the perspectives of students and academic staff, alongside institutional documents. It attends to everyday practice without losing sight of the wider forces that influence it.

At the heart of this work is a practical concern. If an educator wants to make space for African knowledge, what enables that intention to become part of a course, an assessment and the education a student actually encounters? The answer requires attention to both human agency and the institutional contours within which that agency unfolds.

The illusion of change: The unfulfilled promise of decolonisation in a South African private higher education institution

Leburu, P. (2026) · Transformation in Higher Education, 11(0), a684. Published 9 February 2026.

Based on interviews with six lecturers, the article examines how commercial priorities and centralised curriculum governance restrict their capacity to change what and how they teach. It offers a causal account of why decolonial commitments may remain difficult to realise in institutional practice.
